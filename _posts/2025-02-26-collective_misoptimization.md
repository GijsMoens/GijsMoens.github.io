This article is about how groups, organizations, and honestly myself drift toward optimizing the wrong thing — even when everyone involved is smart, well-intentioned, and genuinely trying.

This is not a blame piece. Not about bad incentives or bad people. It’s an attempt to formalize a pattern I keep running into: at work, in conversations with friends, and during those moments where you realize you’ve been “doing well” without being sure *at what*.

Something feels off.  
Not because people are irrational — but because *success itself quietly changes shape*.

---

## The obvious story (and why it’s incomplete)

Imagine a small autarkic village. Each season, Mark faces a simple choice.

He can **extract**  
skip maintenance, overfarm a little, take the short-term win.

Or he can **maintain** the shared land  
repair irrigation, rotate crops, do the slow, unglamorous work.

Individually, extraction makes sense.  
Collectively, maintenance is the only way the village improves.

Economics 101 stops here and says: *align incentives*.  
Add a tax, a subsidy, a rule.

In organizations, this becomes KPIs, bonuses, targets, dashboards.  
In social life, it’s approval, reputation, status.  
In politics, it’s votes, power, ideology.

But the problem was never that Mark is selfish.  
Mark would genuinely prefer the village to thrive.

What’s missing is not motivation — it’s **learnability**.

---

## The hidden assumption: causal transparency

Most optimization arguments assume this:

> If I change my behavior, I can observe the effect.

Let’s give that assumption a name.

**Causal transparency**  
The degree to which you can infer the causal effect of actions from feedback.

Learning by trial-and-error works when:
- the effect is **visible** (something actually changes),
- the feedback is **timely** (it arrives while the action still feels connected),
- the outcome is **attributable** (you can tell *what* caused *what*).

Supermarkets mostly have this property.  
Move a product, sales change.

Social media often does too.  
Post something people like, engagement goes up.

In these environments, *success is obvious* because feedback is obvious.

But many environments don’t work like this:
- hospitals,
- large organizations,
- research,
- relationships.

Here, feedback is delayed, noisy, and confounded.  
The causal signal exists — but it’s hard to isolate.

---

## When feedback stops defining success

From a neuroscience perspective, learning relies on a tight loop:

act → observe → update.

When that loop weakens, the brain doesn’t stop optimizing.

It changes **what counts as success**.

When the true objective is hard to observe, you optimize a proxy — not because it’s better, but because it’s *learnable*.

Throw shoes in a dark room.  
The real objective is *nothing breaks*.  
But you can’t see that.

So you optimize a proxy: *no loud noises*.

That proxy is imperfect — but it responds.

This is **proxy learning**.

It’s not stupidity.  
It’s what intelligence does when reality stops answering clearly.

---

## Epistemic structure (the second axis)

Some systems are dark by nature. You take actions, but you can’t easily tell whether things improved or quietly degraded. **Epistemic structure** is what prevents success from collapsing into guesswork.

> **Epistemic structure**  
The ways a system builds understanding without having to learn by damaging itself.

Instead of learning by breaking things, the system learns by:
- thinking,
- simulating,
- remembering,
- sharing explanations.

If you have a mental blueprint of your room, we could easily rationalize where we should throw the shoe without breaking anything.

Epistemic structure lets systems define success *before* failure makes it obvious.

Common forms:
- accumulated memory,
- simulations,
- protocols and checklists,
- social cues and shared experience.

With strong epistemic structure, proxies stay aligned with what actually matters.  Without it, success gets defined by whatever produces feedback.

---

## The quadrant (for my consultants)

Two axes:
- **Causal transparency**: can I learn from local feedback?
- **Epistemic structure**: can the system learn without breaking anything?

Four regimes emerge.

---

### High transparency, high epistemic structure
Aviation maintenance.  
Making that one joke you know never fails.

You *could* experiment — but there’s no need.  
Success is already well-defined and well-understood.

---

### High transparency, low epistemic structure
The supermarket.  
Figuring out what jokes land with friends.

Immediate feedback.  
Fast, intuitive optimization.  
Success emerges naturally from interaction.

---

### Low transparency, high epistemic structure
Hospitals, nuclear plants, drug development.  
Using a joke you learned from a great comedian because you can’t afford to discover what works by bombing.

Trial-and-error is too dangerous or too slow.  
Success is defined externally, through models, aggregation, and memory.

---

### Low transparency, low epistemic structure
String-pulling organizations.  
Repeating the same joke in different rooms, unsure why it sometimes works.

KPIs detached from reality.  
Confident decisions with weak explanations.  
Optimization continues — but success drifts.

---

## Why this feels so human

This pattern isn’t just organizational.

It shows up in:
- relationships,
- health decisions,
- politics,
- personal life choices.

Whenever feedback is delayed and causality is opaque, humans don’t stop optimizing.

They redefine success as whatever responds:
- attention,
- dopamine,
- approval,
- momentum.

Confidence grows because the system is internally consistent — not because it’s correct.  
From the inside, those feel identical.

> Collective misoptimization is what intelligence looks like when learning is structurally impaired.

And unless a system deliberately engineers transparency or epistemic structure, drift isn’t a failure mode.

**It’s the default.**
