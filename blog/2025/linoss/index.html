Oscillatory state-space models as neurons

Created on February 02, 2025

2025   ·   State-space-models   Neuroscience   Dynamical-systems   ·   Weekly-Thoughts

The idea I keep circling back to:

If an oscillatory state-space model behaves like a neuron at the level of dynamics, then a network of them should pick up brain-like behavior more or less on its own.

Neurons spike, obviously, but that’s just what we get to observe. Internally, neurons seem to spend most of their time in an oscillatory regime: phase, frequency, synchronization, entrainment. If a state-space model lives in that same regime, then spiking starts to look less like the computation itself and more like an output format.

The question that keeps me awake at night:

What if the neuron itself is best thought of as an oscillator?
At this point I’m not even sure if, once you zoom out a bit, almost everything looks oscillatory at the right scale.

The neuron as a dynamical system

At this level of abstraction, a single oscillatory state-space model already overlaps uncomfortably well with how neurons are often described:

  • there’s an internal state evolving continuously in time,
  • it has a characteristic frequency (or multiple),
  • the response to inputs depends on phase,
  • units can synchronize with one another,

That’s basically describing cortical dynamics.

In this picture, spikes aren’t the state they’re an output. A nonlinear, learnable, thresholded, point-process observation of a smooth internal trajectory. The important thing isn’t the spike itself, but the oscillatory state underneath that makes a spike more or less likely at a given moment.

Why oscillatory SSMs feel like a reasonable abstraction

Linear oscillatory state-space models sit in a nice in-between place:

  • the dynamics are structured and stable (no exploding or vanishing as long as eigenvalues behave),
  • phase and frequency are purely degrees of freedom
  • Internally the model stays linear; all the nonlinearity is pushed to the readout, which keeps things simple.
  • long-range temporal structure comes from the dynamics themselves, not memory tricks,
  • inputs naturally shift phase and amplitude,
  • synchrony and desynchrony show up without being hard-coded.

Seen this way, stacking oscillatory SSM layers isn’t really about depth. It’s about coupling multiple oscillatory dynamical systems.

The part that actually matters

If each oscillatory SSM really does behave like a neuron in this limited, dynamical sense, then networks of them naturally support things we usually associate with brains:

  • phase-based coding,
  • transient synchrony,
  • oscillatory gating,
  • entrainment to inputs,
  • resonance-driven selectivity,
  • long-range coordination without explicit attention mechanisms.

All while staying trainable, stable, and scalable.

Or maybe this is one of those ideas that only feels convincing when it’s late, let’s thing about it for one more night ;)
